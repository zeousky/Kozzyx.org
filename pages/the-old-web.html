the old web was messy and good

guestbooks, tiny gifs, strange fonts, and pages made because somebody felt like it

Early personal websites were frequently ugly, inconsistent, slow, confusing, and intensely alive. They were built by people learning in public, not product teams optimizing engagement funnels.

pages felt owned

A personal website could be a diary, collection, shrine, joke, tutorial, fan page, archive, photo dump, or all of those at once. There was no need to fit a platform's content type.

The layout itself became part of the person. Colors, backgrounds, link lists, icons, and strange navigation all communicated taste before you read a word.

links were social infrastructure

Before algorithmic feeds dominated discovery, people found sites through search engines, directories, blogrolls, webrings, forums, and links from other personal pages.

A link was not just navigation. It was a recommendation and a tiny relationship between two parts of the web.

bad design was sometimes good design

Old sites often broke every modern rule: tiled backgrounds, animated GIFs, centered text, tiny hit counters, marquee text, mystery meat navigation, and buttons apparently designed during a power surge.

But the badness was usually specific. A weird site made by one person can be more memorable than an immaculate interface assembled from the same design system as 40,000 other products.

why people are rebuilding it

Personal-site communities, static-site tools, indie web projects, and small hosting platforms have brought some of that culture back.

The appeal is simple: make a page, put it online, link to other people, and stop pretending every act of expression needs a platform strategy.

small facts
  • Webrings linked sites in circular collections around shared interests.
  • Guestbooks let visitors leave public messages before social media comments were normal.
  • Personal homepages were common on early web-hosting services.
  • Static HTML remains one of the most durable ways to publish text online.

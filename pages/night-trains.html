night trains

fall asleep in one city, wake up somewhere geographically inconvenient

Night trains turn transportation into a strange little overnight ritual. You board in the evening, put your belongings into a compartment designed by someone with strong opinions about centimeters, fall asleep while moving, and wake up hundreds of kilometers away.

why they make sense

A sleeper train combines travel time with sleeping time. Instead of spending daylight on an airport transfer, security queue, flight, arrival, and hotel check-in, the journey itself becomes the accommodation.

They can be especially useful between cities that are far enough apart to justify sleeping, but close enough that flying does not save as much practical time as the timetable suggests.

the different levels of comfort

Night trains may offer ordinary seats, couchettes, or full sleeper compartments. Seats are cheapest and also an excellent way to learn that the human spine was not designed for bargain travel.

Couchettes provide simple bunks, often shared. Sleeper cabins usually offer more privacy and proper beds, sometimes with washbasins or private bathrooms.

why they disappeared

Cheap flights, high-speed daytime rail, aging rolling stock, complicated international operations, and low investment all contributed to the decline of night trains in many regions.

Cross-border services are particularly difficult because operators must coordinate timetables, locomotives, staff, track access, and national rail systems.

why they are returning

Interest has grown again as travelers look for lower-carbon alternatives to short-haul flights and a less tedious way to cross long distances.

The romance helps too. It turns out people enjoy transportation that feels like an event rather than a sequence of plastic chairs under fluorescent lighting.

small facts
  • Night trains can cross several countries in a single journey.
  • Some sleeper cabins convert daytime seats into beds at night.
  • Rail travel generally offers city-center to city-center connections.
  • Modern sleeper routes are expanding again in parts of Europe.

mechanical keyboards

a completely reasonable way to spend too much money pressing letters

A mechanical keyboard is, technically, just a keyboard where each key uses its own mechanical switch. In practice it is a hobby capable of convincing normal adults to debate spring weights, plastic blends, lubrication, acoustic foam, and the philosophical meaning of a spacebar.

the switch is the point

Mechanical switches generally contain a moving stem, spring, electrical contacts, and housing. Different switch designs change how the key feels and sounds.

Linear switches move smoothly. Tactile switches provide a noticeable bump. Clicky switches add an audible click because sometimes the office simply has not suffered enough.

layouts

Full-size boards include a number pad. Tenkeyless boards remove it. Smaller layouts such as 75%, 65%, and 60% compress or remove dedicated keys to save desk space.

The smaller the layout, the more likely you are to explain to another person why producing a tilde now requires a minor keyboard chord.

keycaps matter too

Keycaps vary by material, profile, legends, thickness, and manufacturing method. ABS can become shiny with use. PBT generally resists shine better and often has a slightly different texture.

Profiles change the shape and height of each row. This can noticeably affect typing feel even when the switches remain the same.

why people customize them

A keyboard is one of the few computer components you physically touch for hours. Small differences in sound, angle, spacing, and resistance become surprisingly noticeable.

Customization lets people optimize for comfort, aesthetics, noise, portability, or the deeply human urge to turn a solved problem into a hobby.

small facts
  • Hot-swappable keyboards let users replace switches without soldering.
  • Many custom boards support programmable layouts.
  • Switch force is often discussed in grams of force.
  • Keycap sets may cost more than an entire ordinary keyboard, because hobbies fear restraint.

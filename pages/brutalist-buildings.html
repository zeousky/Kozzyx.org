brutalist buildings

concrete, repetition, and the architectural confidence to look mildly hostile

Brutalism is the architecture style people either defend with suspicious intensity or describe as a parking garage that achieved consciousness. It grew after the Second World War and became especially associated with exposed concrete, heavy geometry, repetitive structures, and buildings that make no attempt to pretend they are delicate.

where the name came from

The term is linked to the French phrase béton brut, meaning raw concrete. The point was not brutality in the emotional sense, even if some city halls did eventually look capable of judging your tax return.

Architects used concrete because it was flexible, comparatively affordable, and suitable for large civic projects, housing blocks, universities, libraries, and cultural buildings.

why it looks the way it does

Brutalist buildings tend to show their structure openly. Beams, service cores, stairwells, concrete panels, and repeated modules become part of the visual language instead of being hidden behind decoration.

That honesty can feel powerful. It can also feel oppressive, especially when scale, maintenance, weathering, or poor urban planning turn a bold structure into something cold and inaccessible.

why people hated it

Concrete stains. Large blank surfaces can feel unfriendly at street level. Some projects were built under tight budgets and then neglected, which did no favors to a style already relying on massive gray forms.

Because Brutalism became associated with government buildings and postwar public housing, criticism of the architecture often got mixed together with criticism of bureaucracy, planning policy, and social conditions.

why people love it now

Photography helped rehabilitate Brutalism. Dramatic shadows, repeating grids, huge staircases, and severe forms look fantastic through a lens.

There is also nostalgia involved. Many buildings once dismissed as ugly are now old enough to feel historically specific. Humans do enjoy waiting until demolition is imminent before discovering affection.

small facts
  • Brutalism was especially influential from the 1950s through the 1970s.
  • It was used for universities, government complexes, housing, libraries, and cultural institutions.
  • Exposed concrete is common, but Brutalism is not defined by concrete alone.
  • Preservation fights around Brutalist buildings are now common in many cities.
